Tuesday asked whether first-year writing travels. Today asks you to design for it. We start with your own experience, then move to the assignment you wrote, then build the moves a student would need to carry its learning forward.

01

Transfer Successes and Failures

20 min
01
When did writing knowledge travel for you, and when did it stall?

Analog Sticky notes and the whiteboard, split into two columns: It traveled and It stalled.

Example note · It traveled
"The literature review I learned in a psych methods course gave me the structure for a grant report at my job."
Example note · It stalled
"My five-paragraph essays fell apart in my first history seminar. The professor wanted a question, and I kept giving him a thesis."
  • 1
    Solo (3 min): On two sticky notes, write one moment when something you learned about writing worked in a new setting, and one moment when it failed you. One line each.
  • 2
    Groups of three or four (10 min): Tell your stories. For each one, ask what made the difference. Was it the writer, the teacher, the genre, or the setting? Were you guarding a boundary or crossing one, in Reiff and Bawarshi's terms? What would a teacher have needed to do differently?
  • 3
    Post and read (7 min): Put your notes in the two columns. As a room, name two patterns in the It traveled column. Those patterns become design principles for the next two activities.

Transfer Map for Your Assignment

23 min
02
What does your assignment build on, and where does it point?

Use the Writing Assignment Design you submitted Sunday. A course you teach now works as well. The middle column shows the grid filled in for the Literacy Remix sample from Oct 8. Analog Copy the grid onto paper. Online Add it as a table at the end of your assignment doc.

QuestionWorked example: Literacy Remix (Oct 8)Your assignment
Looking back: what genres and writing knowledge will students bring in?High school personal essays, college application essays, Instagram captions, podcasts they listen to.
Mutt check: where does this genre live outside your classroom, and who reads it there?Memoir, StoryCorps-style audio, and teacher blogs. Readers there listen for a story and a turn, with no thesis required.
Naming: which rhetorical concepts (genre, audience, purpose) does the assignment name out loud?Audience ("beyond our class") and mode. It never names the conventions of a podcast or a photo essay. This is the thin row.
Looking forward: where will students write next, in other courses, jobs, or communities?Personal statements for jobs and graduate school, reflective writing in nursing and education programs.
Evidence: what would show you transfer happened?A transfer note at semester's end, or a short survey the next term asking where students wrote reflectively.
  • 1
    Solo (12 min): Fill the grid for your own assignment. Be specific about real courses and real readers.
  • 2
    Pairs (11 min): Trade maps. Your partner looks for the thinnest row and asks one question about it. Mark the row. It is where your scaffolding goes next.

03

Scaffolding Design Lab

22 min
03
Two moves for teaching transfer explicitly

The Elon Statement on Writing Transfer (2015) calls for "explicitly modeling transfer-focused thinking and the application of metacognitive awareness." A scaffolding move does this work at the scale of one assignment. Three examples to start from:

Before drafting
Genre inventory
Students list three genres they have written before and mark which parts of each help with this task and which parts would mislead them.
During drafting
Two-genre comparison
Students set a sample of the assigned genre beside a sample from a field they plan to enter and annotate where the two expect different moves.
After submitting
Transfer note
A half-page memo to a future self: one thing learned here, the next course or job where it applies, and how it will need to change there.
  • 1
    Solo (10 min): Design two moves for your assignment. Aim at the thin row your partner marked. Borrow from the examples or invent your own. Write each move as the student would see it.
  • 2
    Groups of three (12 min): Read your moves aloud. The group asks: Does this name the transfer out loud, or leave students to find it alone? Does it fit inside the time the course has? Revise one move before you leave. It belongs in your final Digital Portfolio.
