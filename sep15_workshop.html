01

Three Sentences From Your Reading

Full class

Everyone, no discussion leader today, so your Perusall annotations lead instead. The three sentences below are yours, lightly trimmed and unattributed. Read them, then answer the two questions as a room.

Sentence A
"In my master's classes my professors allow students to speak on the same level as them."
Sentence B
"Even the best teachers have to conform to one of these practices because the school district requires it."
Sentence C
"A language cannot be incorrect because its rules exist within it."
Question 1

Which sentence is Freire's claim?

  • Sentences A and B both describe the banking model. They disagree about where it lives. One locates it in what a teacher chooses to do. The other locates it in what a teacher is required to do. Decide which one Freire would recognize, and find the passage in Chapter 2 supporting your answer.
Question 2

What does Sentence C leave out?

  • Sentence C is true, and Anzaldúa would agree with it. Her mother still told her to learn English without an accent because of jobs. What does the sentence fail to say about power? Hold your answer. We return to it in part 03.
02

The Translation Test

Groups of three

Your annotations were strong on what banking looks like in grade school and quiet on what it looks like in a first-year writing course. This part asks you all to translate. Each group takes one ordinary ENG 101 practice from the row below.

Peer review A grading rubric The correctness comment in the margin A grammar quiz The assignment sheet The syllabus attendance policy
In your group

Two questions, written down

  • Where is the banking in it? Answer structurally. Not "a bad teacher would use this badly," but what the practice itself assumes about who holds knowledge and who receives it, whoever the teacher is.
  • What is the problem-posing version? Freire's alternative is not a friendlier version of the same practice. It changes who defines the problem. Describe the practice redesigned so students confront a question the teacher has not already answered.
Report

One sentence per group

  • Name the practice and the single change you made. We will hear all six before discussing any.
  • Then the limitation, which one of you already wrote: "Conforming is almost always easier than creating change." Freire's learners did not choose the institution. First-year students did, and many came for the credential. What does problem-posing mean for people who came for the bank? We will not settle this today. It comes back Thursday.
03

The Office-Hours Question

Pairs

Back to Sentence C. Several of you arrived at the same position in Perusall: no variety of a language is incorrect on its own terms, and judging one by another's rules is imposition. Anzaldúa agrees. The scenario below is where the position gets tested.

Scenario
It is week four of ENG 101. A student comes to your office hours with a draft of the literacy narrative. Parts of it are written in a variety of English the course outcomes do not name, and the student knows it. They put the draft on the desk and ask: "Is this going to cost me?"

Three answers are available in this week's readings. None of them is the safe one.

The descriptivist answer
Your writing is not incorrect. Its rules are its own. This is true. It does not answer the question the student asked, which was about consequences.
Freire's answer
A pedagogy made with the oppressed, not for them. One of you connected this to "nothing about us without us." What would it mean to decide the answer together with the student rather than deliver it?
Wilson Logan's answer
Rhetorical education has never depended on the institution's permission. The literary societies built their own sites. What does the student already know about audience and consequence, and how does the draft show it?
With a partner

Write the sentence you would say

  • One or two sentences, addressed to the student, in the words you would use in the office. Not a policy. What you would say out loud.
  • Under it, write which of the three answers you drew on, or what you drew on instead.
Full class

Hear four or five aloud

  • For each sentence, the same question we used on Anson last week: what does it assume about who has to change?
  • Notice where a sentence protects the student from the institution and where it protects the institution from the student. Thursday's OWI check asks you to make the same distinction about a published principle.
04

Sites You Already Know

Alone, then one line each

The Wilson Logan thread produced the best question of the week, and it came from you all. One of you wrote about English learned from movies, news, and conversation rather than any classroom, and concluded the "sites" Wilson Logan describes are far more scattered than we usually assume. Another asked the question directly.

Your question, from Perusall: "How can teachers change the way they teach writing and communication skills so as to develop and contextualize the rhetorical knowledge students already have?"
Quick write, alone Name one site of rhetorical education your future ENG 101 students already inhabit and your syllabus does not recognize. Be concrete: a platform, a job, a family practice, a community, a game, a form of worship, a group chat. Then one sentence on what they learn there about audience.

We go around the room once. One site each, no commentary. Keep what you wrote. It is the raw material for Thursday: a problem-posing opening built from a site your students already occupy is what makes the problem theirs to define rather than yours to hand over.
