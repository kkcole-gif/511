A Proposal, Not a Plan

Read this first

Everyone, by the end of today you will have three short pieces of writing: a redesigned ENG 101 practice, the sentence you would say to a student who asks whether their language will cost them, and one place your future students already learn rhetoric. Thursday's work starts from the third one.

There is no discussion leader today. Your Perusall annotations lead instead. What follows is my proposal for how to use them. The material comes from you, the problems are ones I have not solved, and the structure is ours to change. Whether it works is part of what we decide, and we will look back at it together at the end.

What I do not know

I do not know which of the three sentences in Part 01 you all will recognize as your own situation. I do not know what you will say to the student in Part 03. I have not answered either question myself in a way I am satisfied with, and I will write alongside you in Parts 03 and 04.

The four parts as proposed
  • Part 01. Full class. We decode three sentences from your annotations: what situation each one describes and from where it was written. Talking only.
  • Part 02. Groups of three. Your group names an ENG 101 practice from your own experience, finds the banking in it, and redesigns it. Two short written answers.
  • Part 03. Pairs. We build the office-hours scenario from the room, work out what each reading lets you say, and write the sentence you would say back.
  • Part 04. Alone. You write two sentences naming a place your future students already learn rhetoric, then post them to the Padlet as the first thing in your section.
The decision we make at the end of Part 01

Keep this order, drop a part, or spend the rest of the session on one part. We vote. The result gets written here on the screen so it stays in front of us:

Three Sentences From Your Reading

Full class · nothing to write

The three sentences below come from your Perusall annotations this week, quoted exactly and without names. Each one is a picture of a situation. We are going to move from the picture to the conditions producing it.

Sentence A
"I have found that in my master classes my professor allow students to speak on the same level as them."
Sentence B
"Even the best teachers have to conform to one of these practices because the general school district requires it."
Sentence C
"A language cannot be incorrect because its rules exist within it."
The moves
  • Move 1. Read all three. Then, for each sentence: what situation is it describing? Not what it claims, but what the writer has seen or lived for the sentence to be sayable.
  • Move 2. From where was each one written? A graduate seminar. A school district. A linguistics classroom. What does each position make visible, and what does it hide?
  • Move 3. Sentences A and B both describe the banking model and disagree about where it lives. Which of them describes your situation as a future teacher of ENG 101? There is no answer I am waiting for. Say which one and why.
  • Move 4. Sentence C is true, and Anzaldúa agrees with it. Her mother still told her to learn English without an accent because of jobs. What does the sentence leave out? Hold your answer for Part 03.
Now the decision

Vote on the proposal. Then write the result in the space at the top of the page and follow the matching link.

The Practice You Name

Groups of three · write two answers

Your annotations were strong on what banking looks like in grade school and quiet on what it looks like in a first-year writing course. The practice you analyze here comes from your own experience, as a first-year student or as a TA, before any list of mine.

The moves
  • Move 1. Form a group of three. Each person names one ordinary thing a first-year writing course made them do, or made them make students do. Something small and routine. Pick one of the three to work on.
  • Move 2. Answer the two questions in the cards below. One person writes.
  • Move 3. Choose one sentence to report: the practice you took and the single change you made. We hear all groups before discussing any.
Question 1

Where is the banking in it?

  • Answer structurally. Not "a bad teacher would use this badly," but what the practice itself assumes about who holds knowledge and who receives it, whoever the teacher is.
Question 2

What is the problem-posing version?

  • Freire's alternative is not a friendlier version of the same practice. It changes who defines the problem. Describe the practice redesigned so students confront a question the teacher has not already answered.
If nothing comes to mind
Peer review A grading rubric The correctness comment in the margin A grammar quiz The assignment sheet The syllabus attendance policy
After the reports, one limitation to hold. One of you wrote: "Conforming is almost always easier than creating change." Freire's learners did not choose the institution. First-year students did, and many came for the credential. What does problem-posing mean for people who came for the bank? I do not have an answer. It comes back Thursday.

The Question in the Office

Pairs · write one or two sentences

Back to Sentence C. Several of you arrived at the same position in Perusall: no variety of a language is incorrect on its own terms, and judging one by another's rules is imposition. Anzaldúa agrees. Here is where the position gets tested, and the test comes from the room.

The moves
  • Move 1. Build the scenario. Has anyone in the room been the student who brought writing in a variety of English the course did not name, and asked whether it would cost them? Or been the teacher asked? If so, and if you are willing, describe the moment in a few sentences. This becomes our scenario. If nobody volunteers, we use the one held in reserve below.
  • Move 2. What does each reading let you say? As a full class, fill the three slots below out loud. What answer does Freire make available? Anzaldúa? Wilson Logan? Say it in one sentence each. I have not filled these in for you.
  • Move 3. Write your sentence. With the person next to you, write the one or two sentences you would say to the student, out loud, in the office. Not a policy. The actual words. Under it, write which reading you drew on, or what you drew on instead. I write one too.
  • Move 4. Hear them. Four or five sentences aloud, mine among them. For each, the same question we used on Anson last week: what does this sentence assume about who has to change?

Where They Already Learn

Alone · write two sentences · post them

The Wilson Logan thread produced the best question of the week, and it came from you all. Two of your annotations, quoted exactly:

From your annotations
"A lot of my English did not come from a classroom at all. It came from movies, books, news, conversations, and just being exposed to the language over time. [...] So maybe the 'sites' Logan is asking about now are much more scattered than we usually think."
From your annotations
"How can teachers change the way they teach writing and communication skills that will help develop and contextualize the rhetorical knowledge that students already have?"
The moves
  • Move 1. Alone, write two sentences. First: one site of rhetorical education your future ENG 101 students already inhabit and your syllabus does not recognize. Be concrete: a platform, a job, a family practice, a community, a game, a form of worship, a group chat. Second: what they learn there about audience. I write mine too.
  • Move 2. Open the Padlet, add a section titled with your name, and post your two sentences as the first thing in it. Your site is now public to the group before Thursday begins.
  • Move 3. We go around the room once. Read your first sentence only. No commentary.
  • Move 4. One question for the room, and I mean it as a question: why would the site you named matter for the opening of a lesson you have not designed yet? Whatever we say is what Thursday builds on.
