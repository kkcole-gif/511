01

Back to Your Outcomes Card

15 min
01
Where does each outcome go after 101?

Materials Your card, sticky notes, one large sheet and a marker per group.

  • 1
    Solo (4 min): Beside each outcome on the front of your card, name where a student will need it after 101: another course, a job, a community. Be specific. "Writing in their major" is too broad, "lab reports in CH 201" works. Put a question mark beside any outcome without a destination.
  • 2
    Same groups as Tuesday (11 min): Pick the outcome whose move on the back of the card felt thinnest. Rebuild the move together, using Tuesday's two versions of Unit 3 below. Does your move name the transfer out loud for students? When does it happen: before drafting, during, or after? On your large sheet, write the outcome at the top, the move beneath it, and where the student takes it next.
Tuesday · Original
Unit 3: Researched Argument
A 5 to 7 page argument on a controversial issue for "a general academic reader," with six scholarly sources, one counterargument, MLA format, a clear thesis, and a conclusion restating the main points.
Tuesday · Rewrite
Make the Case to Someone Who Decides
Students write to whoever holds power over a campus policy, in the genre that reader reads. They list prior genres and the moves they plan to borrow before drafting, then turn in a note on what they borrowed, changed, and found unfamiliar.

Report Out: A Bank of Transfer Moves

15 min
02
When in an assignment does transfer get taught?

The whiteboard holds three columns: Before drafting, During drafting, After submitting. As each group reports, the rest of the room writes the group's move on a sticky note and places it in a column. The board becomes a bank you draw on in the Portfolio Lab.

  • 1
    Groups report (10 min): Post your large sheet. In two minutes, give the outcome, the move, and where the student takes it next.
  • 2
    Read the board (5 min): Which column is crowded, and which is thin? Reiff and Bawarshi's boundary crossers repurpose prior genres while they draft. If the During drafting column is thin, what would a move there ask a student to do?

03

Portfolio Lab

35 min
03
Revise your Writing Assignment Design for transfer

The Elon Statement on Writing Transfer (2015) calls for "explicitly modeling transfer-focused thinking and the application of metacognitive awareness." Draw a move from the whiteboard or from these three, and adapt it to your assignment.

Before drafting
Genre inventory
Students list three genres they have written before and mark which parts of each help with this task and which parts would mislead them.
During drafting
Two-genre comparison
Students set a sample of the assigned genre beside a sample from a field they plan to enter and annotate where the two expect different moves.
After submitting
Transfer note
A half-page memo to a future self: one thing learned here, the next course or job where it applies, and how it will need to change there.
  • 1
    Solo, Tab 4 (5 min): Test your assignment against your card. Which outcome does it serve, and where does a student take that outcome next? Add both as a comment at the top of your assignment.
  • 2
    Solo, Tab 4 (15 min): Write one transfer move into the assignment, worded as your students would see it. Label it "Transfer move" so I can find it.
  • 3
    Pairs (8 min): Read your move to a partner. Your partner asks: When does it happen? What does a student produce? Where does the student say out loud what they are carrying forward?
  • 4
    Solo, Tab 2 or Tab 4 (7 min): If your thinking about transfer has shifted, add two or three sentences to your teaching philosophy in Tab 2, naming one outcome from your card and the move you would now use for it. Otherwise, keep revising your move in Tab 4.
Where this goes Both revisions carry forward into your Composition Pedagogy Statement and Digital Portfolio, due Sunday, December 6.

Bridge to Week 12

10 min

Tomlinson and Newman surveyed 29 autistic writers about how they compose. Their participants named techniques no course had assigned them: playing a "So What?" game to cut what they could not justify, writing phrases on strips of paper and rearranging them, planning the ending first, revising for audience only after drafting. The authors argue instructors "must teach from the students' personal identities outward, rather than from labels inward."

  • 1
    CardMark your moves (4 min): Turn to the back of your card and look at the move you wrote into Tab 4. Mark any move which assumes every student composes the same way, in the same order, at the same pace, in the same setting.
  • 2
    CardName your own technique (3 min): Below the marks, write one technique you use when you write, one no writing class taught you.
  • 3
    Keep the card (3 min): It comes back Tuesday. We open Week 12 with your marks and your techniques.
Carry into Tuesday's reading

This week asked what a student carries out of your course. Dolmage asks who the course was built for in the first place. As you read, ask of the move you wrote today: whom does it welcome in, and whom does it leave to retrofit their own way through?
