Opening

Royster and Young: The Seminar Discussion

~15 min

Tuesday's seminar moves here. Royster and Young both write about linguistic ownership, from different angles: Royster about who gets heard, Young about the cost of code-switching. We start with them as a full class, then you all split into three groups, and we close by putting the groups' work side by side.

Royster, 1996
When the First Voice You Hear Is Not Your Own
Royster asks what it costs a writer, and a listener, when the voice that reaches print is never quite the writer's own, and what changes when we insist on hearing the first voice instead.
Young, 2004
Your Average N****
Young argues against code-switching pedagogy itself. Asking students to leave a home language at the classroom door, he contends, is a demand with a cost, whatever we call it.
Opening Discussion Royster & Young in conversation
Royster asks us to consider "when the first voice you hear is not your own." How does Young's argument against code-switching extend, or complicate, that idea? Where do the two of them agree about what composition owes a writer's home language, and where do they part ways?
  • 1
    Full class (10 min): Work through the opening question together. What would it mean to center linguistic diversity in a course, and is that the same thing Royster and Young are each asking for?
  • 2
    Pick your group (5 min): Read the three group descriptions below and move to the one you want. Aim for roughly even groups. If one fills up, the last people in shift to the smallest group.

Group 01

OWI Design Audit

30 min
01
Whose languages does this task make visible?

Royster and Young both ask us to notice what gets erased when a single linguistic standard passes as neutral. The task below comes from a thoughtful instructor. It offers choice of mode, peer response, and an AI policy. Your job is to find where its stated values and its mechanics pull in different directions.

Sample task ENG 101 Online · Canvas · Unit 2

Unit 2 Project: Literacy Remix

Hi all! For this project, you'll take a literacy moment from your own life, a time when language opened a door for you or closed one, and remix it for an audience beyond our class.

Choose your mode: a 1,200-word narrative essay, a 6 to 8 minute podcast episode with a full transcript, or a captioned photo essay of 10 to 12 images.

Process

  1. Post a 250-word pitch to the Unit 2 discussion board by Friday. Please keep posts in a professional, academic register so we can model clear writing for each other.
  2. Respond to two classmates' pitches using our Praise / Question / Polish protocol.
  3. Before you submit, run your draft or transcript through Grammarly and resolve all flagged errors.

AI policy: You may use generative AI to brainstorm, not to draft. I want to hear your voice.

Rubric

  • Rhetorical awareness30%
  • Development and detail30%
  • Design for your chosen mode20%
  • Clarity and correctness: follows the conventions of Standard Edited American English20%
  • 1
    Read (3 min): Read the sample task on your own. Mark every line where the task tells a writer what their language should sound like.
  • 2
    Audit as a group (10 min): Who does this task center? Where do its promises (your story, your mode, your voice) meet its gatekeepers (register, Grammarly, the rubric)? Which of those gatekeepers would Young call code-switching by another name?
  • 3
    Revise (12 min): Rewrite one element so the task's mechanics match its values. Good candidates: the register instruction, the Grammarly step, or the last rubric line. Write the revision as a student would see it.
  • 4
    Prep your report (5 min): Choose the original line and your revision. Be ready to say in two sentences why the change matters.

Group 02

SRTOL to Syllabus Language

30 min
CCCC, 1974 (reaffirmed 2003, 2014)
Students' Right to Their Own Language
The full 1974 statement with its background document. The resolution itself is a single paragraph at the opening. Read it closely, then skim the background sections for the reasoning behind it.
Open SRTOL (PDF) ↗
02
Writing justice into the syllabus itself

SRTOL is a policy commitment. This group asks what it looks like as prose a student reads on day one.

  • 1
    Read (6 min): Open SRTOL using the link above. Read the resolution paragraph closely and mark the one verb or phrase you would most want a syllabus to carry forward.
  • 2
    Draft on your own (8 min): Write three sentences for a course syllabus that commit the course to something. Skip boilerplate. Write language you would want a student to read on day one.
  • 3
    Trade and revise as a group (10 min): Read each other's drafts. Where does the language make a commitment, and where does it hedge? Revise one draft together until every sentence names something the course will do.
  • 4
    Prep your report (6 min): Pick the three sentences you'll read aloud, and the phrase from SRTOL they carry forward.

Group 03

The DEMAND

30 min
Baker-Bell et al., 2020
This Ain't Another Statement: This is a DEMAND for Black Linguistic Justice
Baker-Bell and co-authors insist on the difference between a statement and a demand. The piece names concrete institutional commitments. Skim the opening framing, then spend most of your reading time on the enumerated demands.
Open the DEMAND (CCCC) ↗
03
What makes a demand a demand?

Your group sets the test for the closing discussion. The other two groups will bring back a revised task and syllabus language, and you all will tell us whether they rise to a demand.

  • 1
    Read (12 min): Skim the framing, read the list of demands closely, and mark the one demand your own institution is furthest from meeting.
  • 2
    Statement vs. demand (8 min): Find one passage where Baker-Bell et al. make the difference concrete. Then put the difference into two or three criteria the room can use.
  • 3
    Structural move (5 min): Name one thing a first-year writing program would have to change, in structure and resources, to honor one demand.
  • 4
    Prep your report (5 min): Bring the passage, your criteria, and the structural move.

Frame

Statement or Demand?

~15 min
Full class Apply Group 03's test
Run Group 01's revised task and Group 02's syllabus language through Group 03's criteria. Does each one rise to a demand, or does it still read as a statement? Then turn the test on SRTOL itself. Where does a 1974 resolution fall, and what would it take to move it?
Take with you

Royster, Young, and Baker-Bell et al. all refuse a version of "neutral" language policy. What would a linguistically just writing course look like if you built it from their refusal from the first day, instead of adding it later as an accommodation?
