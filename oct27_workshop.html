Opening

The Genre You Wrote in First-Year Writing

8 min

First-year composition makes a promise. Writing learned here is supposed to travel into other courses and other jobs. Wardle and Reiff & Bawarshi test the promise from two directions. Wardle looks at the genres the course assigns. Reiff and Bawarshi look at what students bring with them.

Wardle, 2009
"Mutt Genres" and the Goal of FYC
Wardle argues the genres first-year writing assigns often imitate genres from other settings without the audiences and purposes those genres serve elsewhere. She calls them "mutt genres" and asks what the course should teach instead.
Reiff & Bawarshi, 2011
Tracing Discursive Resources
Reiff and Bawarshi follow how students use prior genre knowledge when they meet new writing tasks. Some students hold tightly to genres they know. Others break those genres apart and repurpose the pieces.
  • 1
    AnalogQuick write (3 min): On an index card, name one genre you wrote in your own first-year writing course. On the back, name the next place you wrote anything like it. Leave the back blank if nowhere comes to mind.
  • 2
    Show of cards (5 min): A few of you read both sides. How many backs stayed blank?

Activity

Mutt Genre Check

25 min
A
Where does this genre live outside the classroom?

Below is a familiar first-year writing assignment. Read it through both of this week's lenses. Wardle asks whose genre it is. Reiff and Bawarshi ask what genres a student will reach for to write it. Analog Mark up a printed copy. Online Copy it into a shared doc and comment.

Sample assignment ENG 101 · Unit 3

Unit 3: Researched Argument

Choose a controversial issue and write a 5 to 7 page argumentative research paper taking a clear position on it. Your audience is a general academic reader. Use at least six scholarly sources, address one counterargument, and follow MLA format. Your paper should have a clear thesis in the introduction and a conclusion restating your main points.

  • 1
    Pairs, analyze (8 min): Who is the "general academic reader"? What activity system, in Wardle's terms, does this genre come from, and what does it do there? Which prior genres will students bring to it? The five-paragraph essay? A debate speech? Who here reads like a "boundary guarder," and who like a "boundary crosser"?
  • 2
    Pairs, rewrite (10 min): Give the assignment a real audience and a real place the writing goes. Keep the research. Change the genre, the reader, or both. Write two or three sentences a student would see.
  • 3
    Share (7 min): Three pairs read their rewrites. Is the new version still a mutt? What did the change cost?
Example rewrite "Write a 900 to 1,200 word op-ed on a campus policy you want changed, for Technician, NC State's student newspaper. Cite at least four sources a student reader can check. We will submit the strongest pieces." The research stays. The reader becomes real, and the genre now lives somewhere outside our course.

Discussion

What Counts as Evidence of Transfer?

18 min

Transfer is hard to see. Writing knowledge moves inside a person, across months, into settings a teacher never visits. So every claim about transfer rests on a choice of method. This discussion moves us into the method register.

B
Two readings, two kinds of evidence
  • 1
    Solo (4 min): For each reading, write down what the authors treat as evidence. Find one sentence in each where the evidence is visible. Examples of evidence types to look for: an assignment sheet, a passage from a student draft, a student explaining a choice in an interview, or a survey answer about prior writing.
  • 2
    Full class (14 min): What does each method let the authors see, and what stays invisible? If you wanted to know whether your own course teaches for transfer, what would you collect, and when? How do we prepare students for genres neither we nor they see coming?
