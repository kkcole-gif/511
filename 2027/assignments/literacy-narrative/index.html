You begin the course as its first case. Before you study how writing develops, look at how yours did. The map's caution on reflection sets the standard: it means most when it asks for evidence-based claims about composing choices, and least when it becomes ritual self-disclosure. Make claims you are able to support.

What you produce

  • Literacy Narrative (1000–1500 words, or a 5–7 minute audio or video piece): Choose three or four moments and leave the full chronology out. For each, say what you were asked to write, who read it, and what the response taught you about what writing is. Commitment 2 holds writing is learned through participation in communities. Name yours. Commitment 8 holds no single course teaches writing once and for all. Show where your own development confirms or contradicts it.
  • Teaching Philosophy Draft (500–750 words, Pedagogy Statement tab): Write what you believe about teaching writing now. Cite two or three readings from Weeks 1 through 3 where one changed or sharpened a belief. You revise this draft into your Pedagogy Statement in Week 16, and both versions stay visible in the tab.
  • Format note (audio or video only): Two or three sentences on why the mode fits this narrative.
  • Commitments Close (2–3 sentences, end of the Literacy Narrative tab): Name one or two commitments your narrative illustrates or complicates, and one you doubt. Add what your writing group noticed, or what you chose not to take up and why.
