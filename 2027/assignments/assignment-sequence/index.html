You design one major writing assignment for a first-year writing course, with everything a student needs to complete it: the prompt, one lesson supporting it, and the rubric and feedback plan you would use to respond. It is one object. You build it in three checkpoints and revise it at each.

The design enacts the commitments it tests. Commitment 4 holds composing is recursive, so you return to the prompt after the lesson and again after the rubric. Commitment 5 holds revision is re-seeing, so each checkpoint keeps the earlier version visible. You will not teach the lesson in our class. The design and its rationale are the work.

Checkpoint 1, Week 6: the prompt

Post it to your team by Wednesday, September 29 (Tuesday is Wellness Day). Respond to your teammates by Friday, October 1.

  • Student-Facing Prompt (1–2 pages): Make five things explicit: the rhetorical situation (audience, purpose, genre, medium, stakes), the intellectual task, the evidence practices, the social and disciplinary work the genre does, and the criteria for success. A student should be able to work from it with no further explanation.

The map says when a high-stakes assignment is defensible: when it asks for an intellectually meaningful task and makes those five things explicit. It adds a caution. The assignment should be an occasion for learning, and not only an instrument for sorting, certifying, or catching students. Your teammates read the prompt as a first-year student would and tell you where they stalled.

Week 7: feedback practice, normed with your team

Nothing is due. In class on Thursday, October 7, you respond to two or three sample ENG 101 papers using at least two approaches. Your team then compares what each of you called strong, weak, or unclear. Keep your annotated samples and your notes on where the team disagreed, because Checkpoint 3 draws on both. Open the Week 7 activity.

Checkpoint 2, Week 8: the lesson

Bring it to class Thursday, October 14. Your Workshop Team reviews it in the second half of the session.

  • Lesson Plan (one page): Design one 15-minute lesson preparing students for the hardest move your prompt asks of them. State the commitment the lesson enacts in one sentence. Then give what students will be able to do after 15 minutes, the activity sequence with timing, the materials, and how you will know the lesson worked. Write it so a colleague is able to run it without asking you a question.
  • Revised prompt: Show what your team's reading changed, in tracked changes or side by side.

The map's claim about practice sets the standard: writing improves through structured practice in meaningful rhetorical work, and not through the isolated teaching of correctness. A lesson on comma rules alone does not meet it. A lesson where students revise a paragraph for a named reader does.

Checkpoint 3, Week 13: the rubric and feedback plan

Post both to your team by Tuesday, November 16. Respond to your teammates by Thursday, November 18.

  • Rubric (one page): Write the rubric you would use on this assignment. Test it against Commitment 11 and against Dolmage's question about who a classroom is designed for by default, and mark what the test changed.
  • Feedback plan (150–250 words): Say when students receive response, from whom, and at which of Hattie and Timperley's levels (task, process, or self-regulation). Draw on your Week 7 samples.

Two cautions from the map apply. Rubrics clarify values, and they falsely imply writing decomposes into context-free traits. Feedback must be selective and usable, because commenting on every surface feature obscures higher-order work. Your teammates test the rubric on one of the Week 7 sample papers and report what it rewarded.

The complete sequence, due Sunday, December 5

  • The three pieces, revised: Prompt, lesson, and rubric with feedback plan, each in a form you would use. Earlier versions stay visible below them.
  • Pedagogical Rationale (1000–1250 words): Make one argument about the whole design, in three registers. Activity: what will students do as writers, and where does the sequence leave room for revision? Pedagogy: which commitments does the design enact, and whose rhetorical norms does it treat as the default? Method: what would count as evidence the design worked? Then sort your own choices. Which rest on evidence about effects, and which rest on a justice commitment no effect size settles? Name one source from the field and one from outside it, and say what kind of evidence each offers.
  • Conditions note (150–250 words): Estimate the feedback hours this sequence requires at 15, 20, and 25 students per section. Say what you would change first as the section grows.
  • Commitments Close (2–3 sentences): Name the commitments your design enacts and the one the labor conditions put under most strain. Note what your team's three readings changed.
