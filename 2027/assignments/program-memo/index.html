Written together. One to two pages. Due on your team's assigned Thursday.

Some commitments operate at a scale no classroom document reaches. A lesson plan does not argue for a department's workload policy. The memo does. Your team addresses one named stakeholder, argues one decision, and grounds it in the week's readings.

What each team writes

  • Week 4, Commitment 4 (process): a memo to a WPA (writing program administrator) or department chair arguing how a program should build time for drafting and revision across its curriculum. Ground it in Flower and Hayes and in Graham and Perin.
  • Week 5, Commitments 6 and 7 (use and the disciplines): a short genre report to a WAC (writing across the curriculum) director or a colleague outside composition, arguing what one discipline's writing requires as a way of knowing. Ground it in Carter and Russell.
  • Week 8, the labor condition: a case memo to a chair or dean arguing for one staffing or workload decision. Use CCCC's 15/20/60 standard (15 students preferred per section, 20 maximum, 60 per instructor per term) and Bettinger et al. (2017).
  • Week 10, Commitment 10 (citation): a memo to a curriculum committee arguing how the program should teach citation as a practice of knowledge and credit. Ground it in Logan and Royster.
  • Week 12, the WAC, WID, and CAC condition: a proposal to a WAC or WID (writing in the disciplines) director for one faculty-development session or one writing-center training module. Ground it in Bazerman et al. and North.

The last line of every memo

Close with one line naming the kind of evidence your argument rests on (causal, observational, qualitative, or normative), which of it comes from the field and which from outside it, and where the evidence stops. Where the outside evidence studies another population, such as school-age writers or online courses, say so. The gap is part of where the evidence stops.
