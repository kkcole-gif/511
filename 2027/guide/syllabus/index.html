Course overview

Writing studies is not a field with one uncontested doctrine. Its foundational claims are a contested but durable set of commitments about writing, learning, language, rhetoric, labor, and institutional conditions. This course is built on twelve of those commitments and on three conditions instruction requires. Each week takes one or two. You read the scholarship behind the claim and the critique at its edge, and you practice an activity enacting it.

You will read every claim through three registers. Writing operates as activity (what writers do), as pedagogy (how writing is taught), and as method (how writing produces knowledge). Much of what looks like disagreement in the field is participants arguing from different registers without saying so.

The register predicts the evidence. Claims in the activity register (strategy instruction, feedback, peer response, writing-to-learn) carry causal and meta-analytic support, much of it from outside the field, in educational psychology and the learning sciences. Claims in the pedagogy and method registers (disciplinary enculturation, genre, transfer, class size and labor) rest on qualitative, theoretical, and program evidence. A randomized trial was never the instrument for settling them, and they are no weaker for it. Each commitment's page gives its evidence in two labeled sets, from inside the field and from outside it. The practice this course teaches: place a claim in its register, predict the kind of evidence available for it, then check.

The course prepares you to teach, and its assignments are scaled to the same three registers. You write as a writer studying your own composing (activity). You design one assignment sequence for a classroom (pedagogy). Your team argues from evidence at the scale of a program (method). Five assignments carry the whole course.

Read the twelve commitments and the three conditions. Read the three registers.

How writing is practiced here

The field's durable pedagogical claim: writing improves through structured practice in meaningful rhetorical work, and not through the isolated teaching of correctness or the accumulation of pages. The map names six practices. This course uses each one on you before asking you to use it on your own students.

Practice Where you do it in this course Its caution
Low-stakes writing Perusall annotations every week. In-class freewrites (Week 3). The writing must stay low stakes. Once graded for correctness or monitored, it becomes surveillance in place of exploratory inquiry.
High-stakes writing The prompt in your Assignment Sequence, which you build to make the rhetorical situation, the intellectual task, the evidence practices, the genre's work, and the criteria explicit. The assignment should be an occasion for learning, and not only an instrument for sorting, certifying, or catching students.
Scaffolding The Assignment Sequence, built in three checkpoints. The lesson inside it. Scaffolding should not multiply deadlines into bureaucratic fragmentation.
Peer response and collaborative writing Workshop Teams, all semester. Program Memos. The Week 16 roundtable. Peer response is not self-executing. It requires models, shared purposes, criteria, time, accountability, and attention to power.
Feedback and conferencing The rubric and feedback plan in your Assignment Sequence. Norming in Week 7. Feedback must be selective and usable. Commenting on every surface feature obscures higher-order work.
Reflection and metacognition Every Commitments Close. The tagged sources in your Pedagogy Statement. Portfolio Introduction. Reflection should not become ritual self-disclosure. It means most when it asks for evidence-based claims about composing choices.

Course goals

By the end of the course, you will:

  • State each of the twelve commitments with its limit and its major critique.
  • Place a scholarly or professional claim in its register (activity, pedagogy, or method) and predict the kind of evidence available for it before checking.
  • Distinguish causal, observational, qualitative, and normative evidence, from inside the field and from outside it.
  • Design one assignment sequence (a prompt, a lesson, and a rubric with a feedback plan) enacting named commitments.
  • Give selective, usable feedback and norm your judgments with colleagues.
  • Argue for a program-level decision in a memo to a named stakeholder, stating where your evidence stops.
  • Articulate a teaching philosophy you hold, and show how it changed.
