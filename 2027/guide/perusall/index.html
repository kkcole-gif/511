The four parts of an annotation

You need not label the parts. Let them run together in your comments.

Part 1 · 3 to 6 per reading

Mark a passage and say why

Mark any passage where you stop reading: because it persuades you, troubles you, contradicts a belief you hold, or loses you. Then write a comment saying what caught your attention. Give the page number. A highlight with no comment does not count.

Strong

Berlin holds every rhetoric carries a theory of what is knowable. I keep turning this over. If he is right, current-traditional teaching makes an argument about truth each time it marks a student's sentence, and calling the marking neutral hides the argument.

Too thin

“This is an interesting passage.” Or a highlight with no comment.

Confusion counts. Say where you lost the argument, and say what you took the author to mean before you lost it.

Part 2 · 1 per reading

Summarize in your own words

Somewhere in your annotations, state the reading's central argument in three to five sentences. Use no direct quotation. Write it for a colleague who has not read the piece.

Strong

Fulkerson argues composition in 2005 has no single theoretical center. He sees the field pulled three ways at once: toward expression, toward rhetoric, and toward critique. He prefers a return to rhetoric and says so. What I will keep is his method: he maps what each camp assumes before he argues with any of them.

Too thin

“Fulkerson talks about different theories of composition and how they changed.”

Part 3 · 2 to 3 per reading

Ask open questions

Ask questions you are unable to answer alone. A strong question opens the reading for the room. A question with its answer in the text does less.

Strong

Inoue presents labor-based grading as antiracist practice. I wonder whether the labor a contract values is itself culturally specific. Does showing up mean the same thing for every student?

Strong

Pratt describes autoethnographic texts as sites of power for the writer. What happens to the contact zone once those texts are graded?

Too thin

“Is this still relevant today?”

Part 4 · 1 per reading

Reflect or connect

Tie the reading to your teaching, your own education, another course reading, or a classroom you expect to lead. The week's Reflection Prompt gives you a place to start. Keep it brief and specific.

Strong

Reading Anzaldúa beside my semester as a teaching assistant, I thought of a student who moved between languages in her essays. Her writing was precise, and my supervisor kept marking it unclear. I had no vocabulary for an objection then. I have one now.

Too thin

“This is relevant to teaching.”

Disagreement counts. Say where the reading fails for a required writing course, and give your reason.

One full example

A complete annotation on one passage. The reading is Sommers (1982), “Responding to Student Writing”, assigned in Week 7.

The marked passage. The passage where Sommers reports most teacher comments are interchangeable from one student's paper to the next.

Passage and analysis

I marked this because I recognize my own comments in it. “Be specific” went onto every paper I returned last spring, and it would fit any of them. Sommers's larger argument, as I read it, is about ownership: comments written this way pull a student toward the teacher's purposes and away from the student's own.

Question

If comments teach students what revision is, does a change in how students revise start with a change in what we write in the margin? The usual order puts instruction first and response second. Sommers seems to reverse it.

Connection

For Tuesday: I want to hold this beside Anson (2000). Sommers treats the comment as the problem. Anson treats the reader's idea of error as the problem. I am unsure the two are the same claim.

What strong annotation looks like

Perusall records how fully you annotate, and I read annotations myself. The levels below describe what I look for.

Level What it looks like
Excellent You chose passages for stated reasons. Your comments analyze. Your questions are open. Your reflection ties the reading to teaching or to another reading. You answered at least one colleague's annotation.
Satisfactory The counts are met: 3 to 6 passages, a summary, 2 to 3 questions, and a reflection. Comments stay with what a passage says and stop short of what it implies. Some questions have answers in the text.
Developing Highlights carry little comment. The summary stays close to the author's wording. Rereading would answer the questions. The reflection is general.
Missing No annotations, or annotations posted after class met. I accept late annotations, and they change the labor record for the week. Write to me before the deadline if circumstances stand in the way.

Questions people ask

How long should my annotations be?
No word count applies. A strong set of comments on one reading tends to run 300 to 500 words in total. Shorter works when each comment says something.
Should I answer other people's annotations?
Yes. Answer at least one colleague's annotation on each reading. A short answer works: “I read this differently. I took Berlin to mean...” The answers turn separate annotations into a conversation before class.
I posted late. Does it count?
Late annotations count toward your labor record. They do not help the room prepare, because they were absent when the room needed them. Write to me before the deadline when circumstances stand in the way.
The reading is hard. What do I annotate?
Annotate the confusion, and be specific about where it starts. Naming the sentence where you lost the argument tells the room where to work.
A reading is missing from Perusall. What now?
Every required reading is loaded in Perusall. If one is missing, email me the same day at kkcole2@ncsu.edu.
