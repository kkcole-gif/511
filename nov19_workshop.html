Preparing Your Lightning Talk

10 min

Final presentations are Tuesday, December 1. Each of you gives a 5-minute talk on your portfolio, followed by 1 minute of questions. Five minutes is short on purpose, so the talk follows one thread from your portfolio.

5
A shape for your five minutes
  • 1
    One shift (1 min): A belief about teaching writing you held in August, and what replaced it.
  • 2
    One artifact (2 min): Show one piece of your portfolio where the shift is visible: a revised lesson plan, a rubric row, a line from your assignment design.
  • 3
    One theorist (1 min): The reading most responsible for the change, quoted in its own words.
  • 4
    One open question (1 min): What you are carrying into your teaching unresolved.
Example talk outline Shift: "In August I thought feedback meant fixing errors." Artifact: "Here are my comments on the same paragraph in Week 7 and in Week 13." Theorist: Sommers on teacher comments as they "appropriate" student texts. Open question: "How do I respond to 90 students this way in a 4/4 load?"

Writing as Pedagogy

02

Draft an AI Policy, Then Test It

28 min
02
What does your policy enact, and what does it protect?

Stay in your register groups. Draft a policy for a first-year writing course, using Working Paper 2, McIntyre et al., and the value you wrote on Tuesday. The two examples take opposite positions, and both are written to be read by students on the first day.

Example AUse with disclosure

You may use AI tools to brainstorm, outline, or check citations. Each time you do, add a short note to your submission naming the tool, what you asked it, and what you kept. Drafting your sentences is your work. If a tool writes a passage, quote it and cite it like any other source.

Example BRefusal

This course does not use generative AI. We spend the semester on how your own thinking turns into language, and a tool would skip the part we are here to practice. If a disability or language access need makes this policy a barrier, come talk with me. We will find a path together.

SectionExample from Policy A or B
1. The value"Drafting your sentences is your work."
2. What is allowed, and when"Brainstorm, outline, or check citations."
3. How students account for use"A short note naming the tool, what you asked it, and what you kept."
4. Access and equity"If a disability or language access need makes this policy a barrier, come talk with me."
5. What happens when the policy is brokenNeither example says. Yours should. Example: "We meet and talk through the draft together before anything else happens."
  • 1
    Draft (13 min): Write one sentence per section in the voice a student reads. Analog Draft on chart paper. Online Draft in a shared doc.
ScenarioENG 101 · Week 6

Jordan, a multilingual student, submits a literacy narrative. The draft reads smoothly but sounds nothing like Jordan's in-class writing. In conference, Jordan explains running the draft through an AI tool to "make it sound academic," because a high school teacher had marked their English as wrong.

  • 2
    Test it on Jordan (8 min): Each group takes one theorist: Freire, Inoue, or Baker-Bell. Read Jordan's case under the policy you drafted. Write two or three sentences in your theorist's terms. What does your section 5 do to Jordan, and what would your theorist change in the policy? Example opening for Inoue: "The problem starts with the standard Jordan was trying to meet."
  • 3
    Report (7 min): Each group reads its sentences, or hands them to Prof. Cole to read. Where do the three theorists agree? Which draft protects students' language best, in light of Week 8?
