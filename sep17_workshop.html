03

Problem-Posing Opening

The main work

On Tuesday, Freire's distinction between banking and problem-posing education was the theoretical version. This is the practical one. Your microteaching lesson needs an opening, and this step writes it as a problem-posing move rather than a demonstration of a skill students then copy.

The test. In a banking opening, the teacher already holds the answer and the activity delivers it. In a problem-posing opening, students confront a situation, a text, or a question where the difficulty is real to them and the teacher does not yet know what they will make of it. Ask of your draft: who defines the problem, and is the students' answer able to surprise you?
Part 1

Choose the Skill

  • Name the core ENG 101 writing skill your fifteen-minute lesson will teach. Keep it small enough to fit: integrating a source, revising a paragraph for a reader, moving from summary to response, framing a claim, reading an assignment sheet.
  • Add one sentence on where in a first-year course this lesson sits. Week three of a research unit reads differently from the first day.
Part 2

Draft the Opening

  • Write the prompt exactly as ENG 101 students would encounter it. Address them directly. Include any text, example, or scenario they would need in front of them.
  • Keep it short. A problem-posing prompt is often a single situation and one or two questions. If it runs past a paragraph, you are likely explaining the skill instead of posing the problem.
  • Add one line for yourself, in brackets, on what you expect students to do with it in the first few minutes.
Example of the difference
Skill: integrating a source into an argument

Banking version. "Today we will learn the quotation sandwich. Every quotation needs an introduction, the quote, and an explanation. Here is an example. Now find a quotation in your source and write a sandwich for it."

Problem-posing version. "Here are two paragraphs from last year's students. Both quote the same sentence from the same article. One of them got a note from a reader saying 'I don't see why this is here,' and the other did not. Read them with a partner and decide which one got the note, and what the reader wanted. Then look at your own draft and find the quotation most likely to get the same note."

04

Horning Rationale

One paragraph

Horning argues for critical literacy as the goal of writing instruction, not as an add-on to it. This step asks you all to say, in writing, what your opening has to do with her claim. It becomes the theoretical grounding your lesson plan needs, and it feeds forward into your Writing Assignment Design in Week 10 and the revised teaching philosophy in your final portfolio.

Write one paragraph, roughly 150 to 200 words, as post 3 in your section.

  • 1
    Name the element of Horning's framework your opening enacts most directly. Quote or closely paraphrase her once, with a page number, so your peers and I know where in the introduction you are drawing from.
  • 2
    State what the opening asks students to do with a text or a situation beyond reproducing a form. Be specific about the reading or judgment involved.
  • 3
    Then answer the workshop's original question for your own lesson: if this opening ran in an asynchronous online writing course, what would have to change for the critical literacy to survive? Name one concrete adjustment.
Watch for: rationales describing the skill rather than the literacy. "Students learn to integrate sources" is the skill. What they come to understand about why readers need integration, and who gets to decide when a source belongs, is the critical literacy.
05

OWI Principle Check

Brief

The live version of this session had you all auditing every OWI principle in pairs, sorting which enact justice values and which reinforce institutional norms. We will do the full audit in Week 10, when the assignment is about OWI design. For now, run the audit on your own opening only.

One principle your opening honors
Name it by number and short title. One sentence on how the opening enacts it, and whether the enactment reads as a justice value or as compliance with an institutional norm.
One principle your opening strains against
Name it. One sentence on where the friction is. Do not resolve it here. The friction is often where the most interesting lesson design decision lives, so bring it to the circle.
The critical pedagogy lens matters in the second column. A principle you strain against because it assumes an institutional arrangement your students lack is a different finding from a principle you strain against because your lesson is underbuilt. Say which.
06

Respond to Two Peers

Do this last

This replaces the pair workshopping from the live session. Read the openings of the two people whose sections sit to the right of yours on the Padlet. If you are at the far right, wrap around to the left. If their sections are still empty when you get there, come back later, or leave a comment saying you will.

For each peer

Two Fixed Questions

  • What problem does this pose? Restate it in your own words, as a student would understand it. If no restatement comes, say so. The writer needs to know.
  • Who gets to define it? Point to the place in the prompt where the teacher's answer is already visible, if there is one, or the place where the students' judgment is real.
Form

Keep It Short and Addressed

  • Use the comment field on their post 2, the opening, so your response stays attached to the draft. A few sentences per question is enough.
  • Write to the person, not about the draft. Remember Anson from last week: your reading is one reader's judgment from one context, and it helps to say what context you read from.
  • Do not revise your own opening in response before the circle. Bring the comments with you and we will talk about them together.

A few sentences
Post this as post 5 in your section, in whatever form you choose. A few sentences is plenty if you write it. This is the same question the live session would have ended on, and it is one you all will meet again when you revise your teaching philosophy for the final portfolio.
