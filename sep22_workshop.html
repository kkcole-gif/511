Opening

The Reading Arc

~5 min

Three texts, one movement: from what genre is to what genre does to what genre makes possible. By Thursday you'll be performing a genre yourself — the lesson plan — and McDonald's question will be live in your own work.

Miller, 1984
Genre as social action
Genre is what a text does in a social situation, not what it looks like. The form follows from the recurring situation.
→
Hyland, 2005
Genre-based pedagogy
If genre is learnable, teach it explicitly. The teaching-learning cycle stages the move from model to production.
→
McDonald, 2008
Genres as shapes of meaning
Genre doesn't just shape how you write — it shapes what meaning is available to be made at all.

Your Questions Drive Discussion

~25 min

Your Perusall annotations this week produced thirteen questions in five clusters. The second half of class belongs to those questions. Pick the cluster that's pulling at you most — that's your group.

How it works
  • 1
    Read the five clusters below. Choose the one you want to dig into. Form a group of two or three with others who chose the same one.
  • 2
    In your group (8 min): What is this question actually asking? What does it assume about genre, meaning, or pedagogy? What would Miller, Hyland, and McDonald each say — and do they agree? What does this question mean for how you design a FYC course?
  • 3
    Report back as a full group. Share your question and one thing that came out of your discussion. We'll look for the threads connecting clusters.
01 Genre conventions, creative agency, and innovation
What happens when the action a genre accomplishes is clear, but the rules governing it are not? — Callie
Can words mean anything without people involved? At what point does social context become constitutive of meaning rather than just enabling it? — Anthony
02 Genre, cultural context, and writing across communities
What happens when two groups recognize the same rhetorical situation completely differently? Whose genre prevails? — Hadia
Genre rules are like desire paths — invisible until you're an outsider. How do we make them visible for writers who've never had to notice them? — Hadia, developed by Laura
When we teach academic writing to multilingual students, whose genre assumptions are we transmitting? What gets lost in the move from one language to another? — Grace H.
Email to a professor vs. text to a friend: we shift genres instantly and fluently. What does that lived competence tell us about what genre instruction actually needs to teach? — Emma
03 Pedagogical change, student adjustment, and access
Freirean pedagogy asks students to examine and challenge the systems around them — but rapid pedagogical change produces anxiety in students who were succeeding in the old system. How do we implement transformative pedagogy without destabilizing the students who need stability most? — Kayla L. 3 upvotes
Social knowledge isn't intuitive for everyone — for some students, the implicit codes of genre have to be made explicit as a matter of access, not remediation. How does genre pedagogy account for this? — Kayla B. 3 upvotes
04 Meaning-making: action, form, substance, intention
If McDonald is right that genres shape what meaning is available to be made, does explicit genre instruction expand or constrain what students can say? — Callie, Anthony, Grace P.
05 Genre knowledge, transfer, and students' lived experience
Understanding rhetorical expectations seems like the transferable skill here — more portable than any individual genre. But how do we teach an abstraction? — Nevaeh
Miller says genres hold social situations in place. Does teaching genre literacy make students more agile participants — or better at conforming to something they should be questioning? — Laura
