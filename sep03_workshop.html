Literacy Narrative Share-Out
25 min

Return to your groups from Tuesday. You've already set your listening protocol and planned what you'll share.

In Groups — 18 min
  • Each person shares 2–3 minutes from their narrative: a key moment, or a brief summary of the central tension with a close reading of one scene. Not a reading aloud of the full piece.
  • Listeners hold questions until the person finishes. Then respond using the group's listening protocol from Tuesday.
  • After everyone shares: 3–4 min to notice patterns. What experiences do you share? Where are you strikingly different? What theoretical question does your collective story raise?
Full-Class Report-Back — 7 min
  • One person per group offers 1–2 sentences: the pattern that emerged and the question it raised.
  • Keep it fast. The goal is surfacing 3–4 threads for the rest of the session — not debriefing every group's full conversation.
  • Note the threads on the board — you'll return to what the cognitive model explains and doesn't explain.

Cognitive Models & OWI Design
20 min

Use the Flower & Hayes cognitive process model below as a reference frame for the pair activity. The three components map the composing episode you'll analyze.

Component
Task Environment
External constraints shaping the composing: the assignment, the audience, the writing so far. In OWI: the platform, the interface, the lack of physical co-presence.
Component
Long-Term Memory
What the writer already knows about the topic, audience, and genre. Prior instruction, internalized rules, schemas. Helps and sometimes hinders.
Component
Composing Processes
Planning, translating, reviewing — recursive, not linear. The monitor regulates movement between them. Imbalance produces stuck writing.

Pair or trio activity (12 min): Take one moment from one of your literacy narratives — a specific episode of composing (stuck, flowing, surprising yourself, writing for someone for the first time).

  • 1
    What was the task environment at that moment? What external constraints were shaping the composing?
  • 2
    What long-term memory was active? What did you already know — or think you knew — about writing at that point?
  • 3
    What were planning, translating, and reviewing doing? Were they in balance, or was one dominating?
  • 4
    Given what the model reveals about this episode: design one OWI task that would address the challenge this moment represents. What would a well-designed digital writing task do differently for a writer at this stage?

Brief share-out (5 min): Two or three pairs. What patterns emerge in what the cognitive model reveals — and in what it doesn't explain?

Writer's Block Scenarios
15 min
Rose's framework: Writer's block, Rose argues, is a function of cognitive rigidity — overly rigid or conflicting rules that interrupt the composing process before it can generate. These rules are often internalized from prior instruction and operate below conscious awareness.
Each group works through one assigned scenario. Using Rose's framework, diagnose what is happening in the case — then consider: What would a cognitivist intervention look like? What would an expressivist response suggest? Are they compatible?
Scenario A
Premature Editing
A student writes one sentence, reads it, deletes it, writes it again slightly differently, reads it, deletes it again. After 45 minutes, she has a blank document. When asked what's happening, she says: "I just don't think it's good enough yet."
Diagnose with Rose — then:
▸What rule is operating? Where might it have come from?
▸Cognitivist intervention? Expressivist intervention?
Scenario B
Internalized Prohibition
A student has been told since middle school never to use "I" in formal writing. Now in a first-year college course that asks for personal analysis, he starts every draft with "It is" or "There are" and cannot generate specific claims. His sentences are grammatically correct and informationally empty.
Diagnose with Rose — then:
▸How does a rigid rule produce emptiness rather than error?
▸Cognitivist intervention? Expressivist intervention?
Scenario C
Indefinite Research Deferral
A student says she can't start writing until she's finished reading everything. She has been researching her paper for two weeks, taking notes, and adding more sources. The draft due date is tomorrow. She insists the problem is she doesn't know enough yet.
Diagnose with Rose — then:
▸Is this a knowledge problem or a rule problem? How would you know?
▸Cognitivist intervention? Expressivist intervention?
Scenario D
Perpetual Redraft
A student completes a full draft, reads it, and concludes it is "just bad." He will not revise it. He will, however, start an entirely new draft from scratch. He has done this four times. The new drafts are not meaningfully different from the first.
Diagnose with Rose — then:
▸Why does "starting over" feel different from revising, even when the output is the same?
▸Cognitivist intervention? Expressivist intervention?
Brief Share-Out — 2 min
What's the practical difference between a cognitivist and expressivist intervention? Where does the difference matter most for what the teacher actually does?
Synthesis
Do any moments from the share-out narratives map onto these scenarios? What does that correspondence — or its absence — tell you about the relationship between theory and experience?
