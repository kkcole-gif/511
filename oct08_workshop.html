Opening

Royster and Young: The Seminar Discussion

~20 min

Tuesday's seminar moves here. Royster and Young both write about linguistic ownership, but from different angles — Royster about who gets heard, Young about the cost of code-switching. We open with them before turning to Baker-Bell et al.'s contemporary demand in Activity 03.

Royster, 1996
When the First Voice You Hear Is Not Your Own
Royster asks what it costs a writer, and a listener, when the voice that reaches print is never quite the writer's own — and what changes when we insist on hearing the first voice instead.
Young, 2004
Your Average N****
Young argues against code-switching pedagogy itself, making the case that asking students to leave a home language at the classroom door is not neutral accommodation but a demand with a cost.
Opening Discussion Royster & Young in conversation
Royster asks us to consider "when the first voice you hear is not your own." How does Young's argument against code-switching extend, or complicate, that idea? Where do the two of them agree about what composition owes a writer's home language — and where do they part ways?
  • 1
    Full class (8 min): Work through the opening question together. What would it mean to center, rather than accommodate, linguistic diversity — and is that the same thing Royster and Young are each asking for?
  • 2
    Pairs (8 min): How does Young's essay connect to current debates about linguistic justice you've encountered — in your own teaching, in policy, or in the field? Name one specific connection.
  • 3
    Report (4 min): Share one connection with the room before we move into the workshop activities.

01

OWI Design Audit

~20 min
01
Whose languages does this task make visible?

Royster and Young both ask us to notice what gets erased when a single linguistic standard is treated as neutral. This activity turns that question onto our own course materials.

  • 1
    Pairs (10 min): Review an existing online writing task (your instructor will share one, or bring one from your own teaching). Who does it center? Whose languages and rhetorical traditions are visible — and whose are invisible?
  • 2
    Pairs (7 min): Revise one element of the task to better center linguistic diversity. What did you change, and why?
  • 3
    Report (3 min): One or two pairs share their revision.

02

SRTOL to Syllabus Language

~15 min
02
Writing justice into the syllabus itself

Students' Right to Their Own Language (SRTOL) is a policy commitment. This activity asks what it looks like as prose a student would actually read.

  • 1
    Solo (8 min): Draft three sentences for a course syllabus that enact linguistic justice — not just acknowledge it. Avoid boilerplate; write language you would actually want a student to read on day one.
  • 2
    Pairs (5 min): Trade drafts. Does the language make a commitment, or does it hedge? Suggest one specific revision.
  • 3
    Report (2 min): One or two examples shared aloud.

03

The DEMAND

~20 min
Baker-Bell et al., 2020
This Ain't Another Statement: This is a DEMAND for Black Linguistic Justice
Baker-Bell and co-authors issue a demand, not a statement — a distinction the piece insists on. It names concrete institutional commitments rather than expressions of support.
  • 1
    Pairs (8 min): What's the difference between a statement and a demand? Find one specific passage where Baker-Bell et al. make that difference concrete.
  • 2
    Full class (12 min): What would it look like to honor this demand in a first-year writing program — structurally, not just rhetorically? Connect back to your syllabus language from Activity 02 and your OWI revision from Activity 01: do they rise to the level of a demand, or do they still read as a statement?
Take with you

Royster, Young, and Baker-Bell et al. all refuse a version of "neutral" language policy. What would a truly linguistically just writing course look like if you built it from their refusal, rather than adding it as a late accommodation?
