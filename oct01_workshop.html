01

Summative Feedback: Is Feedback for Judgment?

01
Register 3 · Method
Reading an end comment as a genre
The answer under testFeedback is for evaluating a finished product and justifying the grade.
Carrying in from Perusall

"Is there a way to make end comments have less pressure attached to them? Or is it because of the genre that it will always feel like a weighted comment?"

"How do we as teachers make sure our comments are accessible to students of all knowledge bases?"

Summative feedback makes a judgment about finished work. Smith argues that when we write end comments, we perform a genre with its own conventions and assumptions about what writing is. Reading the genre as a method claim lets you see what a comment treats as evidence, and so what it thinks feedback is for.

Student passageShared text for all three activities
Ever since the beginning of time, people have had to work to survive, and college students are no different. I work twenty-five hours a week at a grocery store while taking fifteen credits. Alot of people think students who work are less serious about school, but I think its the opposite. When I close the store on a Tuesday night and still have a reading due, I have to decide what matters most, and I learned more about time management from that then from any class. My manager says I am one of the most reliable people on the schedule. Which shows that working students bring skills to college that other students don't have. Colleges should give credit for work experience, because work is a kind of learning too.
End Comment AThe conventional genre Smith describes
You have a strong personal connection to this topic, and your example of closing the store on a Tuesday night is vivid. But the paragraph needs more development. Your opening is too broad, your evidence rests on a single personal example, and several errors (alot, its/it's, then/than, a sentence fragment) distract the reader. Your final claim is interesting but unsupported. Work on narrowing your thesis and proofreading carefully. Good start! Stop by office hours if you'd like to talk about revision.B−
End Comment BThe rubber-stamp comment Sommers critiques
Good ideas, but be more specific. Think about your audience. Expand your argument. Watch transitions and grammar.C+

Constructed for this session. Written in the patterns Smith and Sommers document, not taken from their studies.

  • 1
    Solo: Read the student passage, then End Comments A and B. Don't evaluate them yet. Notice what each addresses, what each ignores, and what its structure implies about why it was written.
  • 2
    Pairs: Apply Smith's genre analysis to A, then B. What moves does each comment make? Why does A end with an invitation and a grade? Who is its real audience: the student, the teacher, a future reader of the grade? Is it for the grade, the revision, or the writer's future?
  • 3
    Report (full class): What did the genre reading reveal about purpose that the question "is this good feedback?" would have missed?
What is feedback for, in this comment?

If end comments are a genre, what social action does this one perform, and for whom? Name what it measures, what it ignores, and what a student reading it would conclude feedback is for.

Formative Feedback: Is Feedback for the Writer?

02
Register 1 · Activity Register 2 · Pedagogy
Responding to the writer, not the text
The answer under testFeedback is for the writer's next move, and for teaching them something they carry past this draft.
Carrying in from Perusall

"What comments would let the students understand that growing from their first draft is good?"

"Maybe one answer is not necessarily adding more feedback, but making each round do less."

"How can we ensure this multistep process of feedback and editing occurs during a limited ENG 101 schedule?"

Sommers argues effective feedback addresses the writer's future work rather than the current text's problems. Formative feedback intervenes in the writing process (Register 1) by naming what a writer is doing and what they might try next. That makes it a claim about what writers do and about what instruction should value (Register 2) at the same time.

  • 1
    Solo: Return to the practice passage in Activity 01, setting the end comments aside. Write two or three marginal comments and a short formative note addressed to the writer, not the grade. Name something they're doing well and open one question for revision.
  • 2
    Trios: Read each other's comments, then Formative Comments A and B below, written on the same passage. What does each one assume the writer is trying to do? Which appropriate the text by rewriting it, and which teach?
  • 3
    Report (full class): Where does Sommers' answer hold up? Where does formative feedback slide back into summative judgment?
Formative Comment AFormative in timing, appropriative in practice
Cut the first sentence. Try this instead: "Working twenty-five hours a week while carrying fifteen credits taught me to prioritize under pressure, a skill colleges should recognize." Fix alot, its, and then. Combine the fragment with the sentence before it. Add a second example.
Formative Comment BAddressed to the writer, in the direction Sommers argues for
I kept thinking about your Tuesday night at the store. You're doing something interesting here: using your own schedule as evidence that work produces a kind of learning. That's an argument. What exactly did you learn about deciding "what matters most," and how would a college recognize it? If the paragraph started from that Tuesday night instead of "the beginning of time," what would your reader understand sooner?

Constructed for this session, on the practice passage in Activity 01. Read these after you write your own comments.

What is feedback for, in your response?

What does your response assume this writer needs next? Compare that answer to the ones End Comment A and Formative Comment A give. Where do the two purposes collide?

03

Labor-Based Lens: Feedback for Whom?

03
Register 2 · Pedagogy Register 3 · Method (repudiating)
What would we measure instead?
The answer under testFeedback is for supporting labor and learning, and for refusing to rank writers by one group's language norms.
Carrying in from Perusall

"How do we prepare students for classes that hold more tightly to White language supremacy?"

"I want my students to preserve their voices, but sometimes, those sentences need a little help."

"If a student has to guess what 'clarify' or 'develop' means, then the revision is almost testing how well they can interpret the teacher."

Inoue's argument is a method claim as much as a pedagogical preference. If grading "quality" encodes whose linguistic norms count as evidence of learning, then the question shifts from what feedback is for to whom it serves. This activity turns that question on what you built in Activities 01 and 02.

  • 1
    Pairs: Start with the practice passage, whose writer argues colleges should credit work as learning. Which of End Comment A and Formative Comment B treats the labor the paragraph describes as evidence of learning? Then turn to your own comments from Activity 02. What are they measuring, and whose standard sits behind them? Rewrite your formative note as a labor-based response: what labor is visible in this passage, and what is the next labor? Write it, or record it as a short audio or video comment.
  • 2
    Pairs, extending: One annotation raised a third case: tools like Acrolinx, and now AI checkers, that train every writer toward one house voice. What is that feedback for, and whose standard does it encode?
  • 3
    Full class: Synthesis question, below.
Synthesis question

Activities 01, 02, and 03 give three answers to what feedback is for, and each answer rests on a different account of what counts as evidence that a student is writing. Which answer can absorb the other two's critiques? Which one can't?
