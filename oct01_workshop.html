Summative Feedback

~20 min
01
Register 3 · Method
Reading an end comment as a genre

Summative feedback evaluates a finished product — it makes a judgment. Smith's argument is that when we write end comments, we are performing a genre with its own conventions, purposes, and assumptions about what writing is. This activity asks you to read that genre as a method claim: what does the comment's structure reveal about what the teacher thinks writing is for?

  • 1
    Solo (4 min): Read the sample end comment below. Don't respond to it yet. Notice what the comment addresses, what it ignores, and what its structure implies about the purpose of writing.
  • 2
    Pairs (8 min): Apply Smith's genre analysis. What moves does the comment make? What does it assume the student needs? What does its sequence imply about what the teacher thinks the problem is? What is this comment for — the grade, the revision, the writer's future?
  • 3
    Report (8 min, full class): What did the genre analysis reveal that a pedagogical reading ("is this good feedback?") wouldn't have? What did the comment assume about what writing is?
Text for analysis

Your instructor will project or share an end comment from the ENG 511 Examples folder. Read it before moving to the analysis question below.

Register 3 analysis question

What does this comment assume "writing" is? Name what it measures, what it ignores, and what a student reading it would conclude about what their teacher thinks a paper is for.

Formative Feedback

~20 min
02
Register 1 · Activity Register 2 · Pedagogy
Responding to the writer, not the text

Sommers argued that effective feedback addresses the writer's future work, not just the current text's problems. Formative feedback is designed to intervene in the writing process (Register 1) by naming what a writer is doing and what they might do next. It operates at the intersection of Activity and Pedagogy: it makes a claim about what writers do AND about what instruction should value.

  • 1
    Solo (5 min): Read the same student passage (below) without looking at the end comment from Activity 01. Write a one-paragraph formative response — addressed to the writer, not the grade — that names something the writer is doing well and opens one question for their next revision.
  • 2
    Trios (8 min): Read each other's formative responses. For each one, identify: What does this response assume the writer needs to do? What register (Activity, Pedagogy, or both) is the response working in? Does the response "appropriate" the text — does it rewrite, or does it teach?
  • 3
    Report (7 min, full class): Two or three trios share one response. Class identifies: where is Sommers' argument showing up? Where does formative feedback still default to summative judgment?
Student passage for formative response

Your instructor will share a student passage from the ENG 511 Examples folder. Write your formative response to that passage before comparing in trios.

Register 1+2 analysis question

03

Labor-Based Lens

~14 min
03
Register 3 · Method (repudiating)
What would we measure instead?

Inoue's argument is not simply a pedagogical preference — it is a method claim. Labor-based grading says that measuring "quality" encodes whose linguistic norms count as evidence of learning, and that any grading system is also an epistemological claim about what writing is. This activity asks you to apply that claim to what you built in Activities 01 and 02.

  • 1
    Pairs (7 min): Look at the end comment from Activity 01 and your formative response from Activity 02. For each one, ask: What is this feedback measuring? What would Inoue say that measurement encodes? What evidence of learning would a labor-based protocol use instead — and would that evidence be visible in the same student passage?
  • 2
    Full class (7 min): Synthesis question. The class comes back together for one question only.
Synthesis question

Activities 01, 02, and 03 are not three different opinions about feedback. They are three different epistemological positions — three different accounts of what writing is and what counts as evidence that a student is doing it. Which position can absorb the others' critiques? Which can't?

Feedback Portfolio — Draft Your Frame

~13 min

The last 13 minutes belong to your portfolio draft. The assignment asks you to analyze three pieces of feedback through the tripartite framework. Use today's activities as your working material: the end comment, your formative response, and the labor-based analysis are all candidates.

What the Feedback Portfolio asks

Select three pieces of feedback — given, received, or both — and analyze each one according to which register it operates in, what it assumes writing is, and what it makes visible or invisible. The framing essay (400–500 words) explains why the three examples together constitute a coherent claim about what feedback is for.

Before you leave
Three things to have before Sunday
  • Three feedback examples selected — note which register each one operates in and why you chose it.
  • One paragraph of analysis drafted for one example — follow the register analysis structure from today's activities.
  • A working thesis for your framing essay — name what your three examples together reveal about what feedback is for.
